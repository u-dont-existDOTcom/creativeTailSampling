# An Adult-Led Jurisdiction Map for Inner Work

## Executive Summary

- **Conditional Design Verdict**: IFS explicitly places parts under Self-leadership, with parts providing input while Self retains ultimate decision-making [31]. Schema Therapy assigns the Healthy Adult a parallel role: it validates vulnerable child modes, supports healthy child modes, and sets limits on angry, impulsive, and maladaptive coping modes [14]. -> Use an explicit map only as a short interface contract that resolves conflicts; do not present it as a new internal ontology or as clinically validated constitutional law.

- **IFS Evidence Is Promising but Preliminary**: A 2025 scoping review found **27 studies**, including **17 case studies**, **5 quasi-experimental studies**, **3 qualitative studies**, and only **2 randomized controlled trials** [15]. In the 2026 PARTS trial, both IFS and an active control improved PTSD symptoms, but the difference between arms was not significant [5]. -> Borrow useful process safeguards, but do not infer that the proposed jurisdiction map is effective because IFS has encouraging acceptability findings.

- **Authority Is Not the Same as Voice**: Child states can be authoritative reporters of present feeling, bodily sensation, preference, and need, but their reports are not automatically authoritative conclusions about external danger, historical fact, obligation, or the best long-range action. -> Replace "listen and obey" with "listen, clarify, check, and integrate."

- **Autonomy Needs Structure**: Self-Determination Theory links autonomy, competence, and relatedness with high-quality motivation and engagement [10], while its parenting formulation also values involvement and developmentally appropriate structure and warns that pressure can undermine autonomy support [26]. -> Pair voice and choice with reasons, limits, scaffolding, and a safe plan; do not confuse autonomy support with unlimited avoidance.

- **Avoidance Is the Hard Counterexample**: Exposure literature describes temporary relief from avoidance as negative reinforcement that maintains avoidance and blocks new learning [32]. PTSD research also proposes that avoidance signals that trauma memories are dangerous [7]. -> A state saying "no" should automatically stop optional introspection, but should not automatically cancel every non-dangerous, necessary, values-consistent external action.

- **Safety Review Beats Protector Rule**: IFS recommends assessing protective parts first, respecting their concerns, and negotiating pace [31]. That supports a protector alarm as a circuit breaker and source of risk hypotheses, not as proof that danger exists or as a permanent external veto. -> Require fact-checking, risk assessment, and outside help when appropriate.

- **Care Must Be Unconditional, Behavior Need Not Be**: Schema limited reparenting combines reaching and reassuring the Vulnerable Child with firmness or limits on avoidant and compensatory modes [1]. -> Keep Nurturer care available regardless of compliance, while setting non-shaming limits on behavior, coping strategies, and commitments.

- **Literal Parts and Recovered Memories Create Unnecessary Risk**: The IFS outline treats parts as subpersonalities that may be experienced as thoughts, feelings, sensations, or images [31]. Independent memory research finds that repeated imagination of suggested events can increase false memories [23], and a review identifies therapist beliefs about repression and memory recovery as risk factors associated with suggestive techniques [25]. -> Use parts as provisional perspectives or "as-if" voices, never as authenticated persons, diagnoses, or historical witnesses.

- **The Map Must Earn Its Complexity**: Negative effects of psychotherapy were reported by **25%** of respondents in one systematic review, with dependence on the therapist reported by **12%** and feeling down after therapy by **6%** [24]. ## 1. Decision in Brief: A Map Helps With Conflicts, Not With Truth

The proposed protocol is trying to solve a real design problem: several useful therapeutic intuitions can collide. A child-state may say no; a Protector may predict danger; a Guide may identify a difficult but valuable action; the Nurturer may want to soothe; and the present adult must still pay bills, keep promises, seek medical care, protect dependents, and accept consequences. Without a boundary between these functions, "respect the state" can become either coercive inner parenting or indefinite avoidance.

The strongest answer is therefore conditional. An explicit jurisdiction map adds value as an **interface contract** if it makes four distinctions unambiguous: a report is not a command; consent for optional inward work is not permission to abandon external duties; a safety alarm is not proof of danger; and care is not a reward for obedience. It adds little value if it merely renames the existing Healthy Adult or Self-leadership rule. It becomes actively harmful if the labels are treated as literal agents with independent rights, factual certainty, or authority to compel the person.

The nearest prior art already contains most of the useful architecture. IFS says that Self can lead, that parts provide input, and that protective parts should be approached before vulnerable material [31]. Schema Therapy says that Healthy Adult care and limit-setting operate together [14]. Transactional Analysis describes Adult as responsive to current reality while Parent and Child contain archaic experiences and introjects [3]. ACT offers an even simpler alternative: remain open to internal experience and act in line with values despite discomfort [21].

What remains unanswered is important. The retrieved evidence does not test this particular jurisdiction map against a simpler model. The IFS literature is encouraging but has limited comparative efficacy evidence [15]. The schema-mode study is cross-sectional and explicitly does not establish causal relationships [16]. The evidence about false memory and suggestive therapy is relevant to safeguards but does not prove that IFS or inner-child work causes false memories. The map should therefore be treated as a design hypothesis, not as a discovered fact about the mind.

**Decision:** Use a thin, adult-led map if the existing protocol genuinely has conflicting rules. If a simpler statement already says "feelings and needs are heard; values and facts guide action; safety is checked; care is unconditional," the extra roles are not yet justified.

The comparison shows convergence on adult integration, but not on ontology. IFS and Schema Therapy use differentiated inner roles; TA uses ego states; ACT avoids a role constitution; SDT and shared decision-making emphasize autonomy, structure, and informed choice. This divergence is itself informative: the adult-led function is more stable across models than the names of the parts.

**Decision:** Prefer the least elaborate model that resolves the actual failure mode. Add named jurisdictions only where they prevent a predictable category error.

## 3. A Safer Jurisdiction Map: Five Kinds of Authority, One External Decision-Maker

The word "authority" should be split into different kinds. Otherwise, granting a state authority over a feeling can accidentally grant it authority over a historical fact or a life decision. The following map gives each role a narrow domain and gives the present adult the integration function.

| Role or process | Legitimate authority | Explicitly not authorized | Safer operational response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child-state | First-person report of present affect, bodily sensation, preference, need, and perceived relational meaning. The report is subjectively real even when its interpretation may be mistaken. | Cannot alone determine external danger, factual history, diagnosis, legal or financial obligation, or the whole external life. | Acknowledge the report, ask what need it signals, and offer choices about how to respond. |
| Protector or guard | Can interrupt ordinary deliberation and present an alarm, boundary, prediction, or reason to slow down. IFS specifically supports approaching protective parts first and negotiating pace [31]. | Cannot convert fear into proof, impose a permanent veto on all approach, or decide that every unfamiliar action is dangerous. | Run a safety review: check present facts, severity, reversibility, capacity, alternatives, and whether the alarm reflects current danger or learned threat. |
| Guide | Can propose values, long-range direction, sequencing, practice, and an appropriately difficult step. ACT similarly links values with concrete committed action despite discomfort [21]. | Cannot compel dangerous action, bypass informed consent for inward work, or use moral pressure to silence distress. | Guide proposes; present adult chooses, modifies, schedules, or declines after considering risk and values. |
| Nurturer | Provides warmth, validation, protection, rest, and compassion regardless of whether the person complies. Healthy Adult precedent combines nurture with limits [14]. | Cannot make care conditional on obedience, use tenderness to manipulate compliance, or equate soothing with solving. | Care first or alongside boundary-setting. Separate "you are cared for" from "this behavior is workable." |
| Present adult | Integrates state reports, external facts, values, obligations, professional advice, time constraints, and consequences. | Cannot use the label "adult" to shame, bully, or declare that subjective distress is irrelevant. | Owns external decisions and consequences, explains the rationale, chooses proportionate action, and revises the plan when evidence changes. |
| Optional introspection | The person can pause, refuse, or choose a less activating way to examine inner material. Informed consent in psychotherapy is an ongoing process, not a one-time event [9]. | A refusal of an optional exercise does not automatically cancel external duties or create a factual conclusion. | Stop that exercise, offer alternatives, and revisit only with renewed consent. |

This map improves on a flat "all parts have equal votes" model because it distinguishes **report authority**, **consent authority**, **safety-escalation authority**, **care entitlement**, and **decision authority**. It also prevents the opposite error, where the present adult becomes a monarch who can invalidate all inner experience.

The child-state rule should be stated carefully. "The child is authoritative about how the experience feels now" is useful. "The child is authoritative about what happened then" is too strong. A memory, image, or body sensation can be honored as an important present experience without being treated as independently verified history. The same distinction applies to a Protector: its alarm deserves attention, but its prediction still requires reality testing.

**Decision:** Keep the map only if each role has one positive jurisdiction and at least one explicit prohibition against overreach.

## 4. Failure Modes: The Same Rule Can Protect or Maintain the Problem

### Internal vetoes can reward avoidance

A universal rule that an internal "no" cancels action is unsafe because the short-term reduction in distress can reinforce the refusal. Exposure-based CBT describes avoidance and compulsive behavior as negatively reinforced because they temporarily alleviate distress; this blocks new learning and maintains the fear response [32]. In a PTSD study, the proposed mechanism is that avoiding trauma memories or reminders can signal that they are dangerous [7]. The study found that the interaction between trauma reactivity and avoidant coping was associated with later reexperiencing and numbing for some participants, but it did not support a blanket causal claim that approach coping always improves recovery [7].

The design implication is not "override fear." It is "do not confuse respect with reinforcement." The adult should ask: Is there current danger? Is the task optional or obligatory? Is the refusal about the task, the pace, the method, anticipated shame, or a memory trigger? Could the task be made smaller, more reversible, or more collaborative? If there is no current danger and the action is important, a graded experiment may be appropriate. Trauma exposure should not be improvised as self-treatment; established PTSD guidance recommends specific trauma-focused therapies, including Prolonged Exposure, Cognitive Processing Therapy, and EMDR, while recognizing that not every person chooses or can access them [30].

### Adult leadership can become coercive

The opposite failure occurs when "the adult decides" becomes a license to shame, force, or dismiss. SDT says that developmentally appropriate structure and involvement can coexist with self-determination, but pressure can hinder autonomy support [26]. Informed consent requires information, voluntariness, and decision-making capacity [9]. Shared decision-making requires discussion of options, risks, benefits, and reasons, and it does not mean that a professional must offer an unproved option [8].

For this protocol, adult leadership should therefore be **firm about responsibilities but flexible about method**. The adult may decide that a bill must be paid, a dependent must be cared for, or a clinically indicated appointment must be attended. That does not authorize forced emotional excavation, humiliating self-talk, or the claim that distress is merely a bad child mode. The adult must explain the reason, acknowledge the cost, choose the least harmful workable method, and allow help or renegotiation where possible.

### Reification can turn a useful metaphor into a new problem

The IFS outline says that the mind is subdivided into an indeterminate number of subpersonalities or parts [31]. It also describes parts as experienced through thoughts, feelings, sensations, or images [31]. That can be a useful phenomenological vocabulary. It becomes risky when a person is encouraged to treat a part as a literal independent person, an infallible witness, or an agent with rights that override the present adult.

Memory safeguards are especially important. Experimental work finds that repeatedly imagining suggested events can increase false memories, including high-confidence experiences of remembering [23]. A 2025 review says that therapists' beliefs about blocked or repressed memories are associated with suggestive techniques, while also emphasizing limited data and the need for further research [25]. This is adjacent evidence, not evidence that IFS itself produces false memories. It does justify a strict rule against leading questions, memory-recovery promises, treating imagery as proof, or escalating uncertain narratives into accusations.

### Conditional nurture creates a hidden obedience bargain

If the Nurturer withdraws care whenever the child-state refuses, the protocol reproduces the very conditional acceptance it is supposed to repair. If the Nurturer soothes every refusal and removes every demand, it may instead reinforce avoidance. Schema Therapy's combination of reassurance with limits on avoidant or compensatory modes is a better formulation [1]. The stable rule is: care is unconditional; access to a particular reward, plan, or behavior is not guaranteed.

**Decision:** Every "no" must receive both compassion and classification: danger signal, preference, capacity limit, trauma cue, uncertainty, or avoidance hypothesis. None of those labels should be assigned merely because a state used the word "no."

## 5. Case Studies: Outcomes Favor Process Safeguards Over Role Labels

### Case study 1: The PARTS IFS trial showed acceptability without superiority

The PARTS study randomized **60** patients with PTSD to an online IFS-based group program or an active control, with **30** participants in each arm. The IFS program lasted **16 weeks**, with 16 weekly group sessions and eight biweekly individual counseling sessions [5]. IFS participants attended more group sessions and reported higher group satisfaction, but both arms had significant reductions in PTSD scores and there was no significant difference between arms [5].

This is a useful success and failure case at once. The model was acceptable enough to support attendance and satisfaction, but the trial did not show superiority over a well-matched active control. The PubMed abstract did not report adverse events or dropout data [5]. The result supports measuring engagement and acceptability, not granting IFS or a jurisdiction map special authority.

### Case study 2: The rheumatoid-arthritis IFS trial produced mixed, contact-confounded results

A randomized trial assigned **79** adults with rheumatoid arthritis to IFS or education control. The intervention lasted **36 weeks**, and **82%** of the IFS group completed the protocol [22]. The study reported some posttreatment improvements and some improvements at one-year follow-up, including self-compassion and depressive symptoms, but no sustained improvement in anxiety or disease activity [22]. No adverse effects were reported in the trial [22].

The authors also reported important limitations. The IFS condition involved much more counseling and group contact than the education condition, sessions were not audiotaped for fidelity assessment, attrition was greater in the IFS group, and baseline differences allowed regression toward the mean as an alternative explanation [22]. The case therefore supports a protocol rule that care and relationship may matter, but it does not establish that role differentiation caused the outcomes. A hardened protocol should track contact intensity, adherence, attrition, and adverse effects rather than relying on subjective impressions of inner harmony.

### Case study 3: PTSD guidance combines choice with structured evidence-based options

The VA National Center for PTSD tells patients that they have treatment options, that trauma-focused talk therapies have been studied, and that treatment response varies from one person to another [27]. It also recommends making a treatment plan with a provider and using decision aids to compare options [27]. The VA/DoD guideline recommends Prolonged Exposure, Cognitive Processing Therapy, and EMDR as the best-supported individual trauma-focused psychotherapies, while noting that some people do not choose or cannot access them [30].

This is the right pattern for the proposed Guide and Protector. The patient has meaningful choice, but the menu is not replaced by whatever fear most strongly demands in the moment. The provider does not coerce a specific treatment, but also does not treat reluctance as proof that evidence-based treatment is wrong. Applied internally: the adult offers a rationale and alternatives, the Protector can request a review, and the adult chooses a proportionate next step.

**Decision:** Model the protocol on collaborative, reviewable processes rather than on a hierarchy in which one named state wins every conflict.

## 6. Minimal Rule Set and Rejected Formulations

The following is the smallest rule set that preserves the useful intuitions without making them self-defeating. It is deliberately less elaborate than a full internal constitution.

1. **States may report; the present adult integrates.** Treat affect, sensation, preference, memory, image, and prediction as data. A report is not an order or an external fact.
2. **Optional inward work requires present consent.** A state or the adult can pause an introspective exercise, choose a less activating method, or decline it. No one is required to recover memories or disclose inner content.
3. **A refusal triggers classification, not automatic obedience.** Ask whether the refusal indicates danger, lack of capacity, a boundary, a value conflict, uncertainty, or avoidance. Do not decide the category by role label alone.
4. **Protector alarms trigger a safety review.** Check current evidence, reversibility, severity, time pressure, alternatives, and outside expertise. The alarm gets an interrupt, not a permanent veto.
5. **Guide proposes; the adult commits.** The Guide may identify long-range values and difficult steps. The adult may choose a graded action when risk is proportionate and the action is not dangerous, coercive, or beyond capacity. "Non-dangerous" is necessary but not sufficient; psychological fit and informed choice still matter.
6. **Nurturer care is unconditional.** Care, respect, and protection do not depend on completing the task. Limits may still apply to behavior, coping strategies, spending, substances, commitments, or treatment plans.
7. **External responsibility stays external.** The present adult owns decisions, promises, consequences, and the effects on other people. If the adult cannot safely decide, the adult seeks qualified help rather than delegating to a fictional part.
8. **Use an as-if vocabulary.** Say "a frightened part of me is predicting danger" rather than "the Protector has proved danger." Do not use parts to establish diagnoses, historical facts, or recovered memories.
9. **Review outcomes.** After an action, record what the state predicted, what actually happened, what helped, and whether the rule should change. Use routine outcome monitoring rather than relying on felt certainty [29].

| Rejected or unsafe formulation | Safer replacement | Why the replacement is needed |
|---|---|---|
| "Any internal no is binding." | "Any no must be heard; it stops optional introspection and triggers classification." | A universal veto can be negatively reinforced by temporary relief [32]. |
| "Do not bulldoze guards" means never proceed. | "Do not force unstructured exposure; negotiate pace, method, and support, then test approach when safe and appropriate." | IFS supports negotiated pace [31], while exposure treatment depends on reducing avoidance and safety behavior [32]. |
| "The Protector knows whether danger is present." | "The Protector can raise an alarm; present facts and appropriate expertise determine danger." | Predictions and memories are not automatically external evidence. |
| "The Guide can require action." | "The Guide proposes; the adult decides and owns a graded commitment." | This prevents a supposedly wise part from becoming an internal coercive authority. |
| "The Child decides what happens in external life." | "The Child has voice over feelings, needs, and preferences; the adult decides external obligations." | It protects subjective validity without transferring responsibility. |
| "The Nurturer comforts only after cooperation." | "The Nurturer remains caring while the adult sets limits." | Schema precedent combines reassurance with limits [1][14]. |
| "A part is a literal person or historical witness." | "A part is a useful working perspective unless independently verified otherwise." | Repeated imagination and suggestion can increase false-memory reports [23]. |
| "The adult can override every state because it is rational." | "The adult must acknowledge distress, explain reasons, and select the least coercive workable plan." | Pressure can undermine autonomy support [26], and consent requires voluntariness [9]. |

**Decision:** Implement the numbered rules first. Add role-specific scripts only when a concrete failure remains after these rules are tested.

## 7. Optional Introspection Versus Necessary External Responsibility

The central practical distinction is not between "child action" and "adult action." It is between an **optional inward procedure** and an **external responsibility whose consequences continue while the person reflects**.

| Classification question | Optional introspection | Necessary external responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Self-understanding, emotional processing, imagery, journaling, or experimenting with a parts dialogue. | Health, safety, care of dependents, consent, legal or financial duties, work commitments, relationship obligations, or a time-sensitive decision. |
| What a state refusal does | The exercise pauses or changes. The person can choose not to inspect the material. | The refusal is recorded as important information, but the adult still chooses whether to act, delegate, renegotiate, or seek help. |
| Reversibility | Usually reversible and private, though activation can still be significant. | Delay can create consequences for the person or others; some consequences are difficult to reverse. |
| Appropriate authority | Present consent is decisive for the exercise. | Present adult responsibility, external facts, valid consent of affected people, and appropriate expertise govern. |
| Role of Protector | Ask for pacing, grounding, a different method, or a safety check. | Trigger a safety review and escalation if risk is credible; do not automatically cancel every duty. |
| Role of Guide | Offer meaning, values, and a possible next step. | Offer a plan with alternatives and graded steps; it does not compel. |
| Role of Nurturer | Provide care whether or not the person continues. | Provide care while the adult completes, modifies, delegates, or reschedules the responsibility. |

A useful decision sequence is:

1. Name the proposed action in concrete external terms. "Write the email," "attend the appointment," and "visualize a younger self" are different actions.
2. Ask whether the action is optional, whether another person depends on it, and what happens if it is delayed.
3. Run the Protector safety review. Check immediate danger, coercion, current evidence, capacity, reversibility, and available support.
4. If it is optional inward work and there is no consent, stop or change the exercise. Do not argue that refusal proves a defense or proves a memory.
5. If it is an external responsibility and safe enough to consider, validate the reluctance, explain the rationale, reduce the step, choose a method, and act or delegate. The adult can preserve autonomy by choosing how to meet the responsibility even when the obligation itself cannot disappear.
6. Afterward, compare predictions with outcomes and revise the plan.

This is consistent with the process model of informed consent, in which treatment goals and style can be revisited over time [9]. It also resembles shared decision-making, where preferences and goals matter but acceptable options, risks, benefits, alternatives, and capacity still structure the decision [8]. The analogy should not be overstated: an inner state is not a separate patient, and the protocol cannot use internal dialogue to evade another person's consent.

**Decision:** Consent is a hard boundary for optional introspection. It is not a blanket exemption from external responsibility, and external responsibility is not a license to force introspection.

## 8. Falsification and Outcome Tests for the Map

The explicit map should be compared with a simpler competitor: "Notice feelings and needs; reality-test danger; choose values-guided action; keep care unconditional; the present adult owns external decisions." The comparison can be conducted as a repeated-case or clinician-supervised protocol evaluation, not as unsupervised exposure or a claim of personal diagnosis.

| Domain | Prediction if the map adds value | What would falsify or weaken it | Measurement approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approach versus avoidance | Protector review and Guide planning increase safe approach to valued tasks without increasing harm. | More cancellations, rituals, reassurance-seeking, delay, or safety behavior after a state says no. | Track planned tasks, completed steps, delay, avoidance, safety behaviors, and the state prediction before action. |
| Autonomy and coercion | The person reports more voice, choice of method, rationale, and ability to pause optional work. | Increased pressure, shame, inner conflict, compliance without endorsement, or fear of displeasing the Guide or Nurturer. | Repeated ratings of felt choice, pressure, respect, and endorsement; include qualitative reports. |
| Safety | Protector alarms identify genuine risks earlier without turning ordinary discomfort into danger. | Missed safety problems, escalating distress, impulsive action, self-harm risk, severe destabilization, or a growing number of false alarms that stop functioning. | Record alarm, evidence found, action taken, adverse events, crises, and clinician review where relevant. |
| Reification | The as-if vocabulary improves differentiation and self-compassion without changing reality testing. | Increasing conviction that parts are literal independent persons, inability to make decisions without consulting them, identity rigidity, or novel memory certainty based only on imagery. | Ask about literalness, certainty, agency, and memory status without suggesting answers. |
| Functioning | The adult-led protocol improves ordinary responsibilities and relationships while preserving rest and boundaries. | More missed obligations, dependence on the protocol, isolation, relationship conflict, or inability to act without an internal ceremony. | Monitor work, health, care duties, finances, relationships, and time spent in internal dialogues. |
| Clinical burden | The map reduces confusion enough to justify its extra terminology. | Longer decision loops, repeated jurisdiction disputes, more rumination, or no improvement over the simpler formulation. | Compare time-to-decision, number of steps, perceived clarity, and adherence across matched situations. |
| Well-being and adverse effects | Benefits persist without deterioration. | Symptom worsening, dropout, dependence, feeling down, or other negative effects. One review reported negative effects in 25% of respondents [24]. | Use routine outcome monitoring and review deterioration rather than relying on satisfaction alone [29]. |

Pre-specify the decision rule before using the map. It should be retained only if it produces better or at least no-worse functioning, approach behavior, autonomy, and safety than the simple formulation, while reducing confusion or coercion. A single satisfying internal dialogue is not evidence. Conversely, one difficult session is not proof of harm. Look for repeated patterns and include failure reports.

Use explicit stop and escalation criteria for acute safety concerns, significant deterioration, inability to care for basic needs, escalating self-harm risk, or loss of reality testing. Do not turn these criteria into another internal jurisdiction. They are reasons to involve qualified clinical or emergency support and to suspend experimentation.

The IFS review itself recommends more RCTs, cohort studies, comparative studies, longer courses, and better reporting [15]. That is a direct argument for testing the map as a behavioral process rather than treating its role labels as validated mechanisms.

**Decision:** The primary falsification question is simple: does the map improve safe, self-endorsed action and reduce coercion compared with a simpler adult-led formulation? If not, remove the map.

The most important tension is between protection and learning. IFS and Schema Therapy are right that protective strategies often make sense in context and should not be attacked with contempt [31][14]. Exposure research is right that the same strategy can become a maintaining factor when it is used to escape every safe approach opportunity [32][7]. The solution is not to choose one side. It is to convert the Protector from a ruler into a review process.

A second tension is between autonomy and leadership. ACT, SDT, shared decision-making, and trauma-informed practice all support listening, choice, and respect, but none requires pretending that every preference cancels structure, evidence, or consequences [21][26][8][27]. The present adult should therefore be a responsible agent, not a detached executive and not a passive delegate of whichever state is loudest.

A third tension is between useful metaphor and literal belief. Parts language can make conflicting motives easier to observe and can reduce shame. Yet the evidence on suggestion and memory means that the protocol should never use emotional vividness, imagery, or a part's confidence as proof of history [23][25]. The safest language is phenomenological and provisional.

**Final recommendation:** Adopt a minimal adult-led jurisdiction map only if the current protocol needs explicit conflict resolution. Its added value is not that it discovers separate inner persons. Its value is that it puts four guardrails in writing: subjective reports are heard but reality-tested; optional introspection requires consent; safety alarms trigger review rather than automatic veto; and the present adult owns external action and consequences. Keep Nurturer care unconditional, let Guide propose rather than compel, and test the map against the simpler Healthy Adult/Self/values formulation. If the map increases avoidance, coercion, reification, memory certainty, or decision burden without improving functioning, delete it.

## Project adjudication

- **Strict novelty:** `REJECTED FOR NOVELTY`. Prior art is overwhelming.
- **Formal internal constitution:** reject as unnecessary unless a future comparative test shows added value.
- **Minimal adult-led interface contract:** retain as `PRACTICAL ADDITION / PROVISIONAL DESIGN`, because it resolves actual rule conflicts while remaining falsifiable.
- **Guard re-entry contract:** do **not** require a precommitted retry condition. A clear refusal stops optional introspection now; later revisiting requires renewed consent and a fresh reason to revisit rather than an automatic loop.
- **Protector:** alarm/review trigger, not truth oracle or permanent external veto.
- **Guide:** proposes values/direction/difficulty; does not compel.
- **Nurturer:** care remains available; does not purchase compliance.
- **Present adult:** integrates internal reports with current facts, values, obligations, consent of affected others, appropriate expertise and consequences; owns external action.
- **Parts language:** provisional/as-if/phenomenological; not an authenticated ontology, diagnosis or historical witness.
