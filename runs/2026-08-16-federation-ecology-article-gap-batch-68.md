## Empirical comparison

### Multi-level mutual liability

Kislev, Lerman & Zusman (World Bank, 1989) describe Israeli cooperative agriculture as using mutual liability/guarantee at local and higher-order cooperative levels. Supply cooperatives combined input/service provision with financial intermediation, and higher-order scale/remoteness introduced control problems.

Abramitzky (QJE, 2008) describes pre-crisis kibbutzim as sharing assets/corporations and using movement funds in which kibbutzim could be liable for movement debt in addition to private debt.

Rosenthal & Eiges (Journal of Rural Cooperation, 2014) provide a retrospective crisis/restructuring history.

### Failure coupling

In the World Bank account, regional supply cooperatives that had become financial intermediaries also financed regional service enterprises. Some failing enterprises were supported through additional operating/debt-service finance, so service/credit/ownership coupling transmitted failure upward.

The robust design lesson is not `debt is risky`; it is that **a useful federation service node can become a financial-risk conduit if lending/guarantee authority is allowed to accrete without separate boundaries**.

### Reform

The 1989 World Bank reform architecture proposed that supply cooperatives cease financial intermediation and return to direct supply/marketing services; failing regional enterprises could be closed/sold and member cooperatives would interact more directly with banks.

The kibbutz settlement literature describes progressive constraint/removal of the broad mutual-guarantee system. The exact late-1980s implementation is described at different aggregation levels by different sources, so the run does not claim one single clean switch date. Rosenthal & Eiges identify the 1996 second kibbutz settlement as dismantling the mutual-guarantee system and recalculating repayment capacity per kibbutz.

### Shared-enterprise exit

Rosenthal & Eiges also describe post-crisis regional enterprises requiring clearer owner commitments so that a departing kibbutz's already-committed fixed-cost share did not simply shift to remaining owners and trigger further exits.

This is not a rationale for permanent exit debt. It is evidence for separating:

- already-committed fixed costs;
- future variable/use costs;
- loans;
- guarantees;
- grants/aid.

## Article-gap disposition

### Strengthen G-027 — solidarity is not joint liability

Support-instrument taxonomy:

1. grant/direct aid;
2. pooled reserve/insurance-like protection;
3. loan;
4. guarantee/surety;
5. shared-enterprise capital/fixed-cost commitment;
6. non-transactional exchange/training/relationship transfer.

Each creates different rights and liabilities. `Mutual aid` must not silently authorize a guarantee.

Controls:

- separate approval for guarantees;
- visible contingent exposures;
- caps/collateral where appropriate;
- simultaneous-call stress tests;
- no retroactive conversion of grants into debts;
- ex-ante fixed-cost/exit allocation for shared enterprises.

FEC Batch 66 and kibbutz Batch 68 identify opposite errors: too much parity-accounting can suppress aid, while too much financial coupling can make solidarity transmit liabilities. The target is purpose-specific accounting.

### Strengthen G-024 — separate service authority from financial-risk authority

For every function-specific federation institution distinguish:

- service operations;
- ownership/capital;
- accounts payable;
- lending/borrowing;
- guarantees/surety;
- pooled-risk claims;
- emergency grants.

A service body should not acquire movement-wide credit/guarantee authority by institutional drift.
