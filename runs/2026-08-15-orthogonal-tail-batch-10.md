## T71 — Descendant inflation bias

**Status:** SURVIVED CROSS-DOMAIN CONNECTION.

### Plain proposition

If some community lineages produce many daughter communities and others produce few or none, then sampling **current communities uniformly** does not sample historical community designs/founding attempts uniformly.

A prolific lineage creates many more tickets in the present-day sampling urn.

Example:

- lineage A produces no daughters → 1 extant observation if it survives;
- lineage B produces 20 daughters → up to 21 related observations.

Even if A and B began as one founding experiment each, a contemporary survey can weight B roughly twenty-one times as heavily simply because B reproduced.

### Source-domain mechanism

Branching-process theory describes an **ancestral reproductive bias** or inspection paradox: when a present individual is sampled uniformly and its ancestry traced backward, prolific ancestors are more likely to appear because having more descendants makes them more likely to have a sampled descendant.

Macroevolutionary research identifies closely related sampling artifacts when researchers preferentially analyze large extant clades; diversification-rate estimates can be distorted merely because prolific clades generate more observable descendants.

Relevant source literature:

- Cheek & Johnston, `Ancestral reproductive bias in branching processes` (branching-process inspection paradox).
- follow-up work on ancestral reproductive bias under different sampling schemes.
- Louca et al., work showing diversification-rate/age patterns can arise from preferential sampling of larger extant clades.

### Why ordinary survivorship bias is not the whole issue

Intentional-community researchers have long recognized problems such as:

- short-lived communities disappearing from sampling frames;
- highly institutionalized communities being easier to locate;
- current directories overrepresenting communities that recruit publicly/online;
- living-community samples excluding extinct attempts.

Those are real but distinct.

Even if **every surviving community were perfectly enumerated**, reproductive lineages would still contribute unequal numbers of related observations.

The problem is genealogical multiplication, not merely missing failures.

### Three-step consequence

1. A community trait `T` can increase daughter production without improving local welfare, child outcomes, internal stability, or any other target outcome.
2. Descendants inherit `T` culturally from the parent lineage.
3. A contemporary sample contains disproportionately many `T` communities because that lineage reproduced more.
4. `T` therefore appears highly prevalent among 'successful existing communities' even if it is neutral or harmful for the separate outcome being studied.
5. If descendant communities are also treated as statistically independent replications, confidence in the apparent association can be inflated twice: once through **size-biased representation**, again through **genealogical pseudoreplication**.

### Estimand mismatch

There is no universally correct sampling weight. The appropriate unit depends on the question.

#### Question A — What can a random new founding attempt expect?

Weight **founding attempts/independent roots** appropriately; descendant-rich lineages should not automatically count dozens of times more.

#### Question B — What does a random current community look like?

Uniform weighting of extant communities is legitimate—but the answer is deliberately reproduction-weighted.

#### Question C — What does a random current resident experience?

Weight communities by population, producing another estimand entirely.

#### Question D — What cultural traits are likely to dominate the movement's future?

Reproductive-value weighting may be appropriate because future contribution genuinely depends on descendant production.

Confusing these questions can turn a mathematically correct descriptive statistic into the wrong answer to the research question.

### Why this matters to community research

Suppose a prolific tradition generates fifty daughter communities and a second tradition generates two. A study of fifty-two current communities is not fifty-two independent tests of two institutional designs.

For claims about:

- child happiness;
- internal conflict;
- member welfare;
- governance quality;
- individual freedom;
- sustainability;

researchers may need genealogy-aware estimation rather than allowing reproductive success itself to determine the sample weights.

For claims about **which culture will dominate a spreading movement**, the reproduction weighting may instead be exactly the quantity of interest.

### Operational consequence

Community research datasets should, where possible, add:

- parent/founding lineage;
- daughter relationships;
- independent founding vs organizational offspring;
- common federation/parent support;
- lineage depth.

Then explicitly state the estimand and use an appropriate strategy:

- equal-root / equal-founding-event weighting where appropriate;
- inverse-lineage-size sensitivity analyses;
- hierarchical/genealogy-aware models;
- clustered uncertainty;
- separate estimates for random-community, random-resident, and random-founding-attempt questions.

Do **not** automatically inverse-weight lineages in every analysis; that would simply replace one estimand with another.

### Target-domain screen

Searches found that commune/intentional-community methodology has explicitly worried about:

- undercounting nameless/short-lived communes;
- overrepresentation of highly institutionalized groups;
- convenience/availability sampling;
- directory biases;
- samples containing only living communities.

The targeted search did not locate intentional-community research explicitly treating **daughter-community proliferation itself as a size-biased sampling mechanism** or using lineage weighting/branching-process correction for it.

### Demotion condition

Demote C006 if target-domain methodology is found already making substantially this genealogical-size-bias distinction, especially if parent-daughter proliferation is explicitly incorporated into sampling weights or estimand definitions.

## T76 — Reproductive-memory threshold

**Candidate:** If daughter-founding events occur less frequently than founding know-how is retained, each reproduction becomes effectively a first-time task; regular reproduction preserves reproductive competence.

**Literature collision:** Organizational forgetting explicitly studies skill decay/turnover for infrequently exercised capabilities; crisis-management research emphasizes forgetting during long intervals between rare events. Church-planting practice already emphasizes experienced mentors and repeated reproduction.

**Common-sense compression:** Do something too rarely and people forget how.

**Verdict:** REJECTED AS NOVEL.

Potentially useful empirical variable, but not tail material.

# Batch 10 selection

## C006 survives

The strongest new insight is methodological rather than another social theory:

> **Current-community samples are automatically weighted by historical reproductive success when daughter communities inherit parent traits.**

That creates an estimand problem that ordinary survivorship-bias corrections do not fully solve.

This is especially relevant to the user's broader community research because the project wants to learn several outcomes simultaneously:

- spread/copyability;
- durability;
- child/member welfare;
- governance quality;
- cultural evolution.

A sampling scheme appropriate for one of those outcomes can be wrong for another.

## No other Batch 10 candidate survived

The high rejection rate is intentional. Most source-domain transfers compressed immediately into established target concepts.
